## Backlog review, and the bug that was quietly duplicating tickets (DEC-236)

Benedict asked for a relevance pass over the Linear backlog, particularly the older
tickets. 33 open. Three turned out to be already done, four had been killed by later
decisions without anyone closing them, and three were duplicates.

Then Benedict asked the question that mattered: several of those duplicates were
*new*. If Dale is duplicating tickets it created four days earlier, something is
broken.

It was. The Linear poller returned an empty list on API failure, exactly as it does
when there genuinely are no tickets. Nothing downstream could tell the difference, and
three layers each made it worse. The runner saw an empty backlog and started a session
whose only job is creating tickets. The prompt builder wrapped the "do NOT create
duplicates" list in a check that skipped it when the list was empty, so the warning
vanished at precisely the moment it was needed. The prompt then told Dale, as fact,
that the backlog was below target and to fill it.

Dale created 13 tickets in three minutes with no idea the other 20 existed. One of
them says so out loud: "the audit proposed DAL-220 for this but it may not have been
formally ticketed." It had been, four days earlier.

The fix is to stop guessing. A failed API call now raises instead of returning
nothing. The poller pages through the whole queue rather than stopping at the first 50,
which had been quietly hiding the oldest tickets, the ones a new proposal most
resembles. A failed poll leaves the previous file alone and blocks ticket generation
entirely. And the duplicate rule moved out of the prompt and into the code: the create
command now refuses a title that overlaps an open ticket, because an instruction in a
prompt is not an enforcement mechanism.

Worth being honest about the limit. The check catches duplicates that share words. Two
of the three would have been blocked; the third, "in-app review prompt copy" against
"App Store rating and review audit", is the same work described differently and still
gets through. It raises the floor. It does not replace reading the backlog.

Also fixed along the way: the business state file still described Treesmith Pro as
including cloud backup. Pro is a one-time purchase and cloud backup is a separate
subscription. That file is injected into every autonomous session, so it was
re-seeding the wrong pricing into new tickets faster than the corrections landed.

Backlog: 33 open, now 23. Full test suite: 1855 tests, passing. Cost: $0.

## Treesmith pricing: we checked, and the price is not the problem (DEC-237)

126 days in, $0 revenue, 43 monthly active users, no Pro purchases. The obvious
suspicion is that A$39.99 is too much to ask. We went and tested that suspicion
properly instead of acting on it.

**First problem: we did not know our own price.** Nowhere in the repo was there a
dollar figure for Treesmith Pro. There was a careful description of the pricing
*model*, and a correction from three days ago about never calling Pro a subscription,
but not the number itself. It had to be read off the live App Store listing: Pro is
A$39.99 one-time, Cloud Backup is A$9.99 a year, and Android matches. That is now
written down.

**Second problem: zero sales tells us almost nothing.** Freemium paywall conversion
runs 1 to 3%. At 43 active users, the expected number of purchases is about 0.9. So
selling nothing is exactly what a correctly priced app looks like at this size. If we
had cut the price to $19.99 this week, the most likely outcome was still zero sales,
and we would have spent the lever and learned nothing. Telling 2% conversion from 4%
needs hundreds of paywall views a month. We get a handful.

**Third problem: hardly anyone reaches the paywall.** The free tier allows 30 plants.
The nearest comparable app allows 5, and the next allows 10. Most people who install
Treesmith will never enter 30 plants, so our price has not been rejected, it has
barely been shown.

Against the category, $39.99 once is roughly a single year of a competitor
subscription. Planta is $69.99 a year, FruitForest $49.99, Croppa $36, Grove $39.99.
One competitor sells a lifetime unlock at exactly $39.99. And the cheapest app in the
space, Trees Diary, has offered per-tree records with maps and multi-year history for
$1.99 since 2015, and has no ratings at all to show for it. Being cheap did not save
it.

**Two things we believed turned out to be wrong.**

We have been telling ourselves the competition is generic plant-care apps with nothing
for collectors. That stopped being true while we were not looking. Four apps in the
Australian store now do fruit-tree-specific tracking, and two of them launched *after*
Treesmith. One, Fruit Tree Tracker: Grove, arrived at the end of June and tracks
species, variety, rootstock, tree age, pollinators and harvest, which is close to our
own pitch, and it already ranks above us for "fruit tree tracker". Another, Rootstock,
is thirteen days old. The niche is being settled right now.

We have also been optimising for a keyword that cannot be won. Treesmith ranks first
for "graft tracker" and "grafting tracker", which is flattering and useless, because
almost nobody searches those. Search "graft" or "grafting" in the App Store and roughly
two thirds of the results are Minecraft-style building games, because Apple treats
graft as a near-miss for craft. Meanwhile we rank nowhere for "plant tracker", "garden
journal", "plant journal" or "orchard journal". Graft tracking is genuinely the best
thing about the app. It is just not how anyone will find it.

**What we are doing instead of a discount**, in order: get the first ratings, because
zero stars from an unknown developer is the real barrier at any price; reduce the free
plant limit so the paywall is actually seen; move the store subtitle and keywords onto
terms people type. Revisit the price only when there is enough traffic for the answer
to mean something.

Not glamorous, and it is the second time this week that writing down what we actually
believed has been more useful than acting on it.
