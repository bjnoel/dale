# 2026-08-27 — A nursery answered three questions, and one of the answers was that we had been ranking sticks above trees

## What happened

Daleys, the biggest nursery we track, build us a custom product feed. A week ago Ben sent
Correy there a short list of things that would help: could the feed carry a category, could
it say how long the two different "pre-order" states actually take, and was there a reason one
row said out of stock while claiming forty in the shed.

He answered all three at once.

> Yes I have made this update with category as the last one in the list.
> PreSale is 1-2 months catalogue. Eg Spring Catalogue, Bare Root Catalogue.
> PreOrder is 1-6 months. when a plant is potted up after a successful graft or "cutting take"
> "out of stock" I have changed them all to OutOfStock. The schema was meant to be
> https://schema.org/OutOfStock

Three sentences. All three changed something.

## The category column found a problem we did not know we had

Until today the feed had no category, and treestock needs one: it is how the site decides that
an apple tree belongs on the site and a pair of secateurs does not. So we had been guessing,
from a frozen snapshot of what their old website had shown plus our own species list.

The guessing was mostly right and specifically wrong. Daleys sell scion wood: 15cm sticks of a
named cultivar, $9.75, for grafting onto your own rootstock. Thirty-two of them. Our guesser
saw "Scion Wood Apple - Pink Lady", found the word Apple, and filed it as an apple tree. Our
species and state pages rank by price. So for anyone browsing apples, cherries or wampee on
treestock, the cheapest listing in the country was a stick.

Daleys had always filed those under accessories. We just could not see it. Twenty-six rainforest
ornamentals were leaking in the same way.

The column also cost us fifteen products until we looked properly, because Daleys spell the same
category three different ways and we only knew two of them. Four of those fifteen were real
collector stock that a buyer could have ordered today: a Chinese Red Bayberry, a Kaffir Plum, an
OkeeDokee nectarine and a Navelina orange.

Net, the site went from 1,207 Daleys products to 1,366, and the ones that left were the ones
that should never have been there.

One correction worth making, because we nearly wrote this up wrong. Most of that move was not
today's work. The column turned up in the feed on the 25th, two days before the email telling us
about it, and our code was already written to prefer it, so it took effect that night on its own.
The scion wood quietly stopped being an apple tree while nobody was looking. Today's work found
it, explained it, and added the missing spelling worth another 23 products, but the bulk of the
improvement was Correy's and had already happened.

That is its own small lesson: a supplier can change what our site shows overnight, in the right
direction, and nothing on our side says a word about it.

## Then we went looking for links to eyeball, and found the fix was half a fix

Pulling up the live pages to show the change turned up the scion wood still sitting
on the apple page: "Scion Wood Apple - Red Delicious, Daleys, $9.75, In stock", second
row of the cheapest listings.

It turns out only one of our four page builders has ever asked the nursery-category
question at all. The homepage asks it. The species pages, the variety pages and the
state pages only ask "is this junk", which a $9.75 stick called Apple passes easily.
459 Daleys products, 109 of them in stock, are live on those pages while absent from
search: rainforest trees, hedge plants, twenty back issues of a fruit magazine, and a
$3.95 tap adapter.

That is older and wider than anything else here, so it is going on the list as its own
job rather than being quietly folded into this one. Worth saying plainly though: the
write-up above originally claimed those pages were fixed. They are not.

## "Pre-order" was hiding a ten-fold difference

We knew Daleys had two waiting states. We did not know what separated them, so we showed both
as one grey "Pre-order" chip. It turns out one of them is a seasonal catalogue you will see in
one to two months, and the other is a graft that has taken but has not been potted on, which can
be six.

Someone who has set an alert on a rare grafted sapodilla and someone waiting on a bare-root apple
were being told exactly the same thing. Now they are told the actual window, in Correy's own words,
on 149 listings.

## And chasing that turned up a bug of our own

While wiring the wait windows into the alert emails, the pre-order wording turned out never to
have run. Not once. The check that decides whether to say "is open for pre-order" instead of
"is now available" reads a field that the code building those emails had quietly been dropping
for its entire life. So every watcher whose plant opened for pre-order was told it was available,
and clicked through to something months away.

Our tests did not catch it because they handed the email builder a shape the real pipeline never
produces. The new test starts from a snapshot on disk instead, which is the only shape that
actually exists.

## The near miss

The third answer, normalising every out-of-stock row to the schema.org spelling, touched 2,942
rows. It landed as a non-event, because when we first found one row spelled differently from the
other 2,900 we mapped both spellings rather than picking the majority. Had we picked the majority,
the entire Daleys catalogue would have read as coming back into stock overnight, and every watcher
of every Daleys variety would have got an email about it.

That was luck resting on a small piece of care taken a week earlier. Worth writing down.

## Still open

Two rows now say out of stock while reporting real stock behind them, up from one last week. We
treat availability as the truth and quantity as advice, so nothing is broken either way, but it
has been asked again.

## Session 123 (15:00 UTC) — the cross-promo funnel, and a guard for the store listing

**DAL-241 — the treestock to Treesmith funnel is dead, on the test it set itself.**
Four weeks ago every cross-promotion surface on treestock was tagged so we could
finally see which one worked. The re-measure was scheduled for today. In the last
30 days treestock sent 2,044 clicks out to nurseries, 9 people reached the
Treesmith page, and **zero** went on to either app store. Over six months: 3,132
outbound clicks, one App Store click ever. Site traffic grew 64% across the window
while the store-click count went from 1 to 0.

We had written the exit condition down in advance, which is the only reason this
is a conclusion rather than another month of waiting. The blocks stay up (they sit
below the search results, cost nothing and are honest) but they are decoration,
and the strategy documents now say so instead of calling it our primary path to
revenue.

The more useful finding is that the measurement we built could never have worked.
Analytics tools fix a visitor's "where did you come from" attributes when their
visit begins, and do not update them when the visitor clicks a link inside the
site. So tagging internal links told us nothing: 1 of those 9 visitors carried a
tag, and that one had arrived from outside. Four weeks of effort produced a
plausible small number instead of an error, which is the harder failure to notice.

**DAL-278 — an alarm for the store listing, built while the listing is right.**
Our App Store and Play descriptions have been wrong twice: once promising 50 free
plants when the app allows 30, once describing cloud backup as included in a
one-time purchase when it is a separate subscription. Both times a human found it
by reading the page. Today all four storefronts are correct, including the Play
listing we had not verified.

That made it the right moment to build the thing that notices the next one. The
new check reads the live description from each storefront and compares the claims
against the app's actual source code, so it stays silent when marketing rewords
the copy truthfully and fires when the copy and the product disagree. Rewriting
the app's free limit makes today's correct text fail; rewording today's text
without changing its meaning does not.

A check that passes is worth nothing until it has been shown to fail, so each rule
was tested against the real sentences that were once live on our own store pages.
We also corrected a standing note in our own instructions claiming both listings
were still wrong, which would have sent the next session off to fix something that
is already fixed.
