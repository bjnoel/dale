# 2026-09-17: the first referral commission, and a word for "not now"

## What happened

treestock earned its first money. A collector clicked through from treestock to
Primal Fruits in Perth and placed an order, and Primal's affiliate program paid a
5% commission: A$32.89. It is booked in the ledger.

It matters more as proof than as income. Until now we only assumed our referral
tag survived the trip to a nursery's checkout. A real order says it does. Ranking
on treestock still ignores commission entirely, and the affiliate disclosure page
still lists every nursery that pays us, which is one.

## Tidying the relationship register

Two nursery conversations went quiet: one after a friendly visit and a thank-you,
one after an email asking permission to list them. Ben decided not to chase either
for now.

The register had no way to say that. It could say "we wrote and are waiting",
which invites a follow-up, or "they're engaged", which was no longer true. So it
now has a seventh status, `cold`. A reply warms it back up, and writing to them
again marks it as waiting. Silence is not treated as consent or refusal, and both
nurseries stay listed.

## The gap in the App Store numbers, and which way it leaned

Apple's analytics for the app had been frozen since 20 August. The report we
were subscribed to was a one-off snapshot, so it produced its data once and then
stopped, and every weekly summary since had been reading the same day back to us.
Ben approved a standing subscription instead, and today's job was to confirm the
new one actually works before closing the question.

It does. But confirming it turned up something else.

Apple hands its data over in daily bundles, and each bundle holds three days. We
had been reading only the newest bundle, on the assumption that the older ones
were just earlier drafts of the same thing. They are not. They are a window that
slides forward a day at a time, so reading only the newest one works fine as long
as you never miss more than two nights in a row. Miss a third and those days fall
off the end and nobody can get them back.

That had already happened. Our records jumped from 20 August straight to 10
September, with nothing in between and no error raised anywhere, because a day we
simply do not have and a day when nobody looked at the app add up to exactly the
same number: zero.

We now read every bundle Apple still holds, which recovered five of the missing
days. Fifteen days are gone for good.

The part worth writing down is what the missing days were hiding. It would have
been easy to file this as housekeeping. Adding those five days back moved our
estimate of how often the app appears in search from 72 times a day to 59,
because the days the fault happened to keep were the busy ones. So for two weeks
we had been overstating the effect of the app's rename by about a fifth, in the
one measurement the rename is being judged on, in the direction that made our own
decision look better.

A gap in a record does not average itself out. It picks a side, and we had no way
of knowing which until we went and got the missing days. The report now says out
loud when days are missing from a period rather than quietly averaging across the
hole.

Where that leaves the rename, honestly: 36 appearances a day in the four weeks
before, 59 in the eleven days we hold since. Eleven days is not a trend, and
appearing in search is not the same as being downloaded. The rank re-measurement
that was scheduled for yesterday is still the test that settles it.
