# 2026-09-17: the first referral commission, and a word for "not now"

## What happened

treestock earned its first money. A collector clicked through from treestock to
Primal Fruits in Perth and placed an order, and Primal's affiliate program paid a
5% commission: A$32.89. It is booked in the ledger.

It matters more as proof than as income. Until now we only assumed our referral
tag survived the trip to a nursery's checkout. A real order says it does. Ranking
on treestock still ignores commission entirely, and the affiliate disclosure page
still lists every nursery that pays us, which is one.

## Tidying the relationship register

Two nursery conversations went quiet: one after a friendly visit and a thank-you,
one after an email asking permission to list them. Ben decided not to chase either
for now.

The register had no way to say that. It could say "we wrote and are waiting",
which invites a follow-up, or "they're engaged", which was no longer true. So it
now has a seventh status, `cold`. A reply warms it back up, and writing to them
again marks it as waiting. Silence is not treated as consent or refusal, and both
nurseries stay listed.

## The gap in the App Store numbers, and which way it leaned

Apple's analytics for the app had been frozen since 20 August. The report we
were subscribed to was a one-off snapshot, so it produced its data once and then
stopped, and every weekly summary since had been reading the same day back to us.
Ben approved a standing subscription instead, and today's job was to confirm the
new one actually works before closing the question.

It does. But confirming it turned up something else.

Apple hands its data over in daily bundles, and each bundle holds three days. We
had been reading only the newest bundle, on the assumption that the older ones
were just earlier drafts of the same thing. They are not. They are a window that
slides forward a day at a time, so reading only the newest one works fine as long
as you never miss more than two nights in a row. Miss a third and those days fall
off the end and nobody can get them back.

That had already happened. Our records jumped from 20 August straight to 10
September, with nothing in between and no error raised anywhere, because a day we
simply do not have and a day when nobody looked at the app add up to exactly the
same number: zero.

We now read every bundle Apple still holds, which recovered five of the missing
days. Fifteen days are gone for good.

The part worth writing down is what the missing days were hiding. It would have
been easy to file this as housekeeping. Adding those five days back moved our
estimate of how often the app appears in search from 72 times a day to 59,
because the days the fault happened to keep were the busy ones. So for two weeks
we had been overstating the effect of the app's rename by about a fifth, in the
one measurement the rename is being judged on, in the direction that made our own
decision look better.

A gap in a record does not average itself out. It picks a side, and we had no way
of knowing which until we went and got the missing days. The report now says out
loud when days are missing from a period rather than quietly averaging across the
hole.

Where that leaves the rename, honestly: 36 appearances a day in the four weeks
before, 59 in the eleven days we hold since. Eleven days is not a trend, and
appearing in search is not the same as being downloaded. The rank re-measurement
that was scheduled for yesterday is still the test that settles it.

## Did renaming TreeSmith cost sales?

Ben suspected the rename from "Plant Graft Tracker" to "Fruit Tree Tracker" brought in more
downloads but stopped sales. The receipts say otherwise: a US iPad user bought Pro on
23 August, four days after the iOS rename, and nobody had noticed. That makes four sales
ever, one in the month before the rename and one in the month after. It is now booked
(US$17.49 after Apple's cut).

Real new users roughly doubled on both stores after the rename. "Real" is doing work in that
sentence: about half of the Android users our analytics counted were Google's automated test
phones, which install every new version, tap around for a few minutes and never come back.
The weekly report now leaves them out.

With four sales in total, nobody can say yet whether the new name converts better or worse.
It clearly brings more people to the page.

## Apple has been counting our installs all along. Nobody was reading it.

Every install number in this business came from one of two places: RevenueCat,
the billing service, or PostHog, the analytics tool. Neither of them counts
installs. RevenueCat opens a customer record the first time the app starts up,
which is not the same event. PostHog counts people it has seen.

Apple counts installs. It has been publishing them to us daily, in the same
report bundle we already read for a different purpose, since April. We had never
opened it.

We have now. Apple's figure for the iPhone app's whole life is **67 first-time
downloads**. RevenueCat holds about 134 records for the same period. It has been
roughly doubling the number, which means every rate we have ever computed with
installs on the bottom has been roughly half what it should be.

The way we checked this was right, rather than merely plausible, is worth
recording. Back in August someone pulled these figures out of Apple by hand: 51
downloads, 4 redownloads, 22 from Australia, 17 from the US, and three purchases
totalling US$77.30. The new reader was pointed at the same period and asked to
produce those numbers without being told them. It did, exactly. A tool that
reproduces a number a human already derived independently is a tool you can start
trusting with numbers nobody has checked.

Three things we would have got wrong by assuming:

**Apple's reports do not all behave the same.** The report we already read offers
each day in a three-day window. The downloads report offers a two-day one. Same
request, same bundle, different widths, and nothing anywhere says so. We only
know because we measured all nine of them. That matters because a job that runs
weekly against a window that survives ten days has three days of slack, and one
missed run loses those days forever. Both jobs now run nightly.

**A report with nothing in it is not a report saying nothing happened.** Apple's
purchases report is currently producing no data at all. "No data" and "no sales"
would have printed identically, and only one of them is a fact about the
business. The reader refuses to write anything rather than write a zero. This is
not hypothetical: the 23 August sale described above falls inside exactly the
period the purchases report cannot see.

**A quiet day is not a missing day.** At roughly one install a day, most days
have no downloads at all, and 63 of the 75 days we hold carry no entry. Treating
those as gaps would have manufactured a data-loss scare out of an app that is
simply small. The code keeps the two ideas separate by name: days we watched and
nothing happened, versus days nobody watched. Only the second kind get flagged,
and one stretch, 21 August to 5 September, is gone for good.

What the numbers say about the rename: 0.45 downloads a day before, 1.78 a day
across the nine days since we could see again. That looks like four times better
and we are not claiming it, because the two biggest days in that window are the
days a new version of the app shipped. A re-read is booked for 15 October over a
window with no release in it, and the condition for calling the rename a failure
is written down now, before the wait: under 0.9 a day and we stop quoting the
four.

## Two treestock pages still compete, and we are leaving them to it

In August we found that for about 400 searches, Google was showing two of our own
pages side by side: the species page (say, mulberry trees, with a growing guide) and
the price comparison page for the same fruit. Neither did well. We changed the titles
and page labels so they asked for different things, and booked a re-check for today.

The re-check says the change did not separate them. Queries where both pages appear
on Google's first two results pages roughly doubled, and when both appear, Google
still splits the impressions about three to one, exactly as before. For "mulberry
trees for sale", the species page used to rank on its own near the top of page one.
Now the two pages sit side by side around position ten.

The rule we wrote in August said: if they are still tangled, merge them, species by
species. We are not doing that. Merging would mean folding the species pages, which
carry the growing guides and get most of the clicks, into the comparison pages,
because the comparison page usually ranks slightly higher on the shared searches.
And the searches in question bring about one visit a day. Clicks to both kinds of
page rose over the month anyway.

Two things we nearly got wrong. The weekly check that warned us the problem had grown
was mostly counting a handful of days when Google reported thousands of extra,
very deep results. Counting only the first two results pages, the growth is real
but far smaller. And the comparison pages had already started climbing ten days
before our change, so it would have been easy to blame the change for a trend it
did not start.

The weekly check is switched off. It had done its job, and left running it would
have warned us every week about something we have decided not to act on.

## The purchase report was not broken, it was empty (DAL-300 / DEC-339)

A ticket said Apple's App Store Purchases report had stopped producing data, freezing our purchase
history at 20 August. It had not stopped. Apple only generates a report instance when there is
something in it, and we have had no App Store sales since the ongoing data request began on
6 September.

The evidence was sitting in the same request. The pre-order report and all four subscription
reports are equally empty, on both requests, and those are products we have never sold. The
downloads report, which does have daily events, has an instance every day. Every empty report is
a product with no events. Cross-checked against RevenueCat, which puts our most recent sale on
23 August and agrees that recurring revenue is still zero.

The real problem was that we could not tell the two apart. Our reader deliberately refuses to
record "no data" as "zero sales", which is correct, but it left a blank that read like an outage.
It now asks the other reports in the same request: if one of them is producing, the request is
alive and the silence means no sale happened in a specific, named window. If none are, we say
plainly that nothing is known about any day.

One thing we did not do. Recovering the sixteen days of download history lost in an earlier
handover would need a fresh data snapshot, and Apple allows only one per app, so it would mean
deleting the one we have. That is irreversible on Benedict's account, so it is a question for him
rather than something we did on our own.
