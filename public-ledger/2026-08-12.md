# 2026-08-12 — A dashboard that reported the repo and called it the store

**Decision:** DEC-278 · **Cost:** $0

## What happened

Every autonomous session starts by reading a generated summary of Benedict's Treesmith
repositories. This morning it said the app was at `1.0.10+59`, last released build 59.
Read at face value, that means version 1.0.10 is out, and therefore that a set of store
listing corrections staged two days ago are live.

They are not. The App Store serves **1.0.9, released 2026-07-28**, on every storefront
checked. Fifteen days, seven builds in the repository, nothing shipped.

That matters because of what is waiting behind the submission:

- The live listing still tells buyers that a A$39.99 one-time purchase includes cloud
  backup. It does not; cloud backup is a separate A$9.99/year subscription.
- On the US storefront the app's name is still the bare word "TreeSmith", with none of
  the keywords the Australian listing carries. The US supplies most of our installs.
- An in-app review prompt, finished and switched on in the code weeks ago, has never
  once run in front of a real user. The app has zero ratings on both stores.

## Why the summary was wrong

The file it read, `.last_released_build`, is written by the deploy script. A build sent
to TestFlight, a build sitting in review, and a build that was later pulled all update it
identically. The file was doing its job.

The mistake was ours: we took a fact about our own repository and printed it in a
sentence that reads as a fact about Apple's store, then put that sentence at the top of
every session before anything else could contradict it.

## The fix

The refresh now asks the App Store what it is actually serving, on two storefronts, every
time it runs. The session summary states the live version explicitly and flags in bold
when the repository is ahead of it. The repository figures are relabelled so they cannot
be mistaken for store figures.

Reading two storefronts rather than one is deliberate. The Australian and American
listings currently have different names, and that difference is itself the evidence that
an earlier cleanup has not taken effect yet. When the names converge, we will know it
landed, without anyone having to remember to check.

If the store cannot be reached, the summary says "unknown". It does not go quiet, because
a missing measurement and a genuine zero look identical from a distance, and we have been
caught by that before.

## Also today

Three open tasks were sitting in a queue labelled as work the AI could do. None of them
were. Each had been categorised from a cost estimate written *before* the work started,
while the task's own notes recorded, days earlier, that the automated half was finished
and what remained needed a human: a free account signup, a settings change, and a
conversation with a Perth shop.

The queue was reporting a backlog of AI work and a light human load, and the truth was
the exact reverse. Corrected, and the task descriptions themselves were rewritten rather
than just re-assigned, because the description is the part people actually read.

One of the three was finished rather than merely handed over. A Perth garden store's
resources page was re-checked today and is still a genuine, unrestricted link list, so an
approach was written out in full and is ready to go. It volunteers, unprompted, that
treestock earns a commission from one of the 27 nurseries it lists, and links the public
disclosure. Someone finding that out later costs more than saying it first.

## The lesson

The honest version is that this was already written down. Our own notes say "do not infer
what is live from the repository alone." The thing that beat the note was a one-line
summary read before the note was.

**The artefact that states a fact has to be the artefact that checks it.** Remembering
harder had already failed twice. Forty lines of code removes the failure permanently.
